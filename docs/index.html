00 — Abstract

Abstract

With thousands of content pages and limited reviewer time, which pages deserve a human's attention first? This report frames that as a ranking problem — Lane 2 of the FlyRank ML Internship — and builds a client-grouped Random Forest model that scores pages by likelihood of decline, using only signals observable before the decision point. The model is compared against a transparent, hand-written baseline rule on the exact same held-out clients and the same metric, Precision@50. Across two independent client-grouped evaluation runs, the model scored 0.66 and 0.56 respectively, both several times higher than the baseline's 0.28 — a directional, decision-support result, not a fixed benchmark. Two leakage checks — one at the feature level, one at the split level — are reported in full, including a case where an apparently "honest" score of 0.999 turned out to be an artifact of low-volume noise. The final output is a ranked, human-reviewed action playbook with reason codes, an explicit no-go list, and monitoring triggers, built for one honest purpose: helping a reviewer spend limited time on the pages most worth it.

01 — Introduction

Introduction & Problem Statement

FlyRank's client portfolios contain tens of thousands of content pages each, and traffic to those pages moves constantly — some pages grow, some quietly decline for months before anyone notices. A content team can only review a small number of pages per week. The question this report answers is not "which pages are declining" in the abstract, but a narrower, more useful one:

This is the real problem behind two of FlyRank's own product flags studied during this track: a CTR-fix flag (pages underperforming their search position) and a refresh flag (pages worth revisiting after a long gap). Both exist because someone at FlyRank already had to solve this exact triage problem by hand. This report is a case study in building and honestly validating one version of that triage logic from first principles — checking the signals behind those flags before trusting them, then testing whether a learned ranking earns its keep over a transparent rule.

QUESTION

Which pages should a content reviewer open first, this week, given limited capacity?

This is a decision-support ranking problem, not a classification problem in the usual sense — the deliverable is an ordered queue with reasons attached, not a single yes/no verdict. That framing shapes every choice in this report: the metric (Precision@50, matched to a realistic weekly review capacity), the validation design (client-grouped, so the model is judged on clients it has never partially seen), and the final output (a playbook with an explicit list of what a human must check, and what should never be automated).

Why this month, and why not the newest data

A mid-panel month (March 2026) was chosen deliberately over the release's final month. The warehouse's _sample table holds exactly the last month of history — using it to build or test label logic would mean training on the same window a real deployment would later try to predict, an easy way to fool yourself into an optimistic result. The final month was treated as a sealed test window and left untouched throughout this project.

What was excluded, and why

EXCLUDED

fact_content_query_90d (the query-level table) was deliberately left out of this pass. It's a genuinely useful table for future work — query concentration, rare/anonymized tail share — but including it here would have made the first data contract too large to verify carefully. Scope was kept small on purpose, not because the table lacks value.

LIMITATION

The panel is unbalanced across clients — gsc_data_start differs per client, so this one-month slice contains full prior history for long-tenured clients and little or none for newer ones. Any month-over-month comparison needs a per-client history check before it can be trusted.

03 — Methodology

Methodology

Label

The target, is_declining, flags a page whose second-half-of-month impressions fell more than 20% below what the first half would project:

is_declining = imp_h2 < 0.8 × avg_impressions_h1 × 15

This is a proxy label — a same-month, percentage-threshold definition, not a verified business outcome. A stronger capstone-grade label would use a longer forward window and a matched, non-percentage-based threshold; this is named explicitly as future work in Limitations.

Features (all first-half-only)

FeatureAvailable at decision point because…
avg_impressions_h1Sum of Mar 1–15 impressions only — already observed
avg_clicks_h1Same first-half window
ctr_h1Ratio of two first-half quantities — no second-half information
avg_position_h1Average search position, first half only
days_with_impressions_h1Count of visible days within the first 15 days only

Baseline

A transparent, hand-written rule, ctr_below_tier_expectation: flag pages in the top_10/top_20 position tiers whose CTR sits meaningfully below the tier's own observed average, scored by ctr_gap × impressions. This mirrors the CTR-fix logic behind FlyRank's own product flags, and its two supporting signals were checked against real bucketed data before being trusted — both came back CONFIRMED.

Model

A client-grouped Random Forest classifier (300 trees, max depth 8, balanced class weight), chosen because no single signal above correlates strongly with the label in isolation (all under 0.10) — exactly the shape of problem a single rule structurally can't capture, but a model that combines several weak signals can.

Validation design

Pages are split 75/25 by client (GroupShuffleSplit), with zero client overlap confirmed between train and test. A plain random split would let pages from the same client sit in both halves, letting the model partly recognize a client's style rather than learning general signals — the honest test is on clients the model has never seen a single page from.

Leakage checks

LEAK FOUND — FEATURE LEVEL

Deliberately including imp_h2 — the exact quantity the label is built from — as a feature spiked ROC AUC to 0.999. On first pass, even the "honest" feature set (without imp_h2) still scored 0.999, because a low minimum-volume floor let percentage-threshold noise on tiny pages leak signal through avg_impressions_h1 alone. Raising the volume floor from 50 to 500 impressions fixed it — the honest score dropped to a real 0.614.

LEAK FOUND — SPLIT LEVEL

A naive random split (ignoring client boundaries) scored 0.82 Precision@50; the honest client-grouped split scored 0.56 on the identical features and label. The 0.26-point gap is the size of the optimism client leakage was buying for free.

Errors and interpretation

Both built-in and permutation feature importance agree that ctr_h1 drives the model most (built-in: 0.331, permutation: 0.057 — several times larger than any other feature in either ranking). avg_clicks_h1 and avg_impressions_h1 showed slightly negative permutation importance, meaning they're largely redundant with ctr_h1, which already combines both.

The model's false positives share a clear pattern: very poor position (avg position in the high-30s/40s) combined with near-zero CTR — pages that are already performing badly rather than pages actively getting worse. This is a real limitation, addressed directly in the final playbook (Section 06) with a dedicated monitor_only action for exactly this pattern, rather than silently rewriting pages that have nowhere left to fall.

On result stability

OBSERVED

A later, independent re-run of the same client-grouped evaluation (as part of the Week-6 validation audit) scored 0.56 instead of 0.66 — both several times the baseline's 0.28, but not identical to each other. With a modest number of held-out clients, the exact figure moves run to run. This range, not a single number, is the honest result.

05 — Limitations

Limitations & Honest Framing

SCOPE

Built and evaluated on one mid-panel month (March 2026) of a 104-client slice. Not yet tested against the sealed final month, other months, or the full multi-year warehouse history.

LABEL

is_declining is a same-month, percentage-threshold proxy, not a verified business outcome. It says nothing about whether a page's decline matters commercially, only whether its impressions fell by more than 20% within one month.

CAUSALITY

Nothing here shows that acting on a flagged page — rewriting a title, refreshing content — causes recovery. This mirrors a finding from an external audit performed on FlyRank's own published research paper as part of this track: a reported "52× impression lift" from refreshing old pages was not randomly assigned, so the gap may partly reflect which pages editors chose to refresh, not what refreshing itself does. The same caution applies here: this model ranks candidates worth a human's attention, it does not prove a fix will work.

VARIANCE

Precision@50 moved from 0.66 to 0.56 across two honest re-runs of the same method — a real result, but not a fixed benchmark, given the modest number of held-out clients in a one-month slice.

GENERALIZATION

The 0.56–0.66 range is an average across held-out clients. Any single new client could sit well above or below it, especially one whose content style differs a lot from the training set.

Human review, before acting on any flagged row

  • Is a title/meta change already in progress for this page?
  • Is the low CTR intentional — a snippet written to filter low-intent clicks on purpose?
  • Is there a seasonal explanation for a temporary dip likely to reverse on its own?
  • Does this page belong to a client with very little training history? Treat with extra caution.
Never automate
  • Auto-publishing rewritten titles or metadata without human sign-off
  • Auto-merging or redirecting pages flagged as overlapping — a structural decision beyond this model's training
  • Treating a high decline probability as proof that a specific fix will work
  • Scoring clients or content types absent from training data without re-validating first

Monitoring & retrain triggers

  • Re-score a fresh month's client-grouped holdout monthly; if Precision@50 drifts down toward the baseline's 0.28, retrain.
  • Track the monthly mean of ctr_h1 and avg_impressions_h1 across the scored population — a large shift means the tier-expected-CTR reference is stale.
  • If the overall decline rate moves sharply from ~50%, revisit the label threshold itself, not just the model.
  • Exclude any client with less than one full month of history from scoring until they cross that bar.
