FLYRANK SEARCH INTELLIGENCE — RANKING SIGNAL ANALYSIS

Why Word Count and Ranking Position Don't Predict Content Decline

A signal audit of 30,000 pages across 32 clients

Abstract. We asked which content and search signals are associated with a page's search performance declining, and whether commonly-assumed signals — article length and current ranking position — hold up once obvious confounders are controlled for. Using a 30,000-page cross-sectional dataset spanning 32 clients, we ran verdict-style bivariate tests followed by a controlled logistic regression, then validated a simple predictive model with a client-grouped cross-validation split. Both word count and ranking position looked meaningful in raw comparisons but lost statistical significance once content type, intent, content age, and traffic volume were held constant. Content age and traffic volume were the two signals that persisted. A logistic model built on the signals that survived controls beat a naive majority-class baseline by five points of precision at the top 500 ranked pages, using an honestly validated split that avoided client-level memorization. We frame every result as observed and decision-support, not causal, and recommend against using word count or position alone to prioritize content review.

Introduction

Content teams routinely treat two numbers as trustworthy shorthand for page health: how long an article is, and where it ranks. The working assumption is intuitive — longer, more thorough content should hold up better, and pages already ranking well should be safer bets. This matters for a concrete decision: when a content ops team has limited time to review pages for refresh or rewrite, which signals should decide the queue?

We test that assumption directly against real search performance data, using a 90-day trailing window across a diverse set of client sites, and ask whether the popular story survives contact with confounding variables that a casual glance would miss.

Data

This analysis uses the FlyRank ML Internship starter dataset: 30,000 rows, one per pseudonymized content item, across 32 pseudonymized clients, with metrics aggregated over a trailing 90-day window ending at export. It does not draw on the larger ~79 million-row warehouse release, which requires gated Hugging Face access this analysis did not have — see Limitations for what that scoping choice costs.

Excluded from this analysis: trend_direction and trend_pct as features (they define the label itself), provider_used / model_used (generation metadata, not a search signal), and any row with avg_position == 0 from position-based tests (zero means "no position data," not position zero).

Methodology

The outcome is is_declining_label: 1 when a page's impressions dropped more than 20% from the prior 30-day window to the most recent 30-day window (54.2% of pages, the base rate against which every result below is measured).

Candidate signals — word count, content age, days since last update, ranking position, and search intent — were chosen because each is something a content team could plausibly act on, and none is derived from the label. Confounders held fixed: content type (missingness in word count tracks content type exactly, confirmed before any imputation), search intent, content age, and log-transformed traffic volume.

Validation used 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation grouped by client, rather than a random split, because rows from the same client share hidden characteristics a random split would let a model memorize. The random-split AUC is reported alongside the grouped number specifically to surface the size of that gap.

Taken alone, both patterns run backwards from the common story — longer content and better rankings look worse, not better. A plausible read is a ceiling effect: pages already at the top or already long have less room to grow and more room to mean-revert downward. But neither number controls for anything else yet.

A logistic regression including content type, intent, content age, and log-traffic alongside word count and position told a different story:

Model vs. baseline

A transparent rule baseline — "flag old, low-traffic pages first," no fitted weights — actually performed below the majority-class floor. A logistic model built only on the signals that survived controls did modestly better than both:

Limitations & honest framing

This is a cross-sectional snapshot, not an experiment. Nothing here shows that changing word count or position would cause a change in decline risk — only that, in this data, they are not associated with it once confounders are held fixed. All claims in this paper use observed, measured, or decision-support language; none are causal.

  • Scoped to the 30,000-row starter slice, not the full ~79M-row warehouse — no time-series panel, no per-client history check, no sealed future-month test. A true time-aware split is the natural next step and was not what was run here.
  • The model's lift over base rate is real but modest (+5.3 points of precision@500) — read as directional, not strong.
  • The rule baseline underperformed the majority-class floor; reported as-is rather than tuned to look better.
  • 32 clients only; GroupKFold folds may not represent the full diversity of client behavior in the larger warehouse.

Ranked recommendations

  1. Prioritize review queues by content age and traffic volume — the two signals that held up under controls — not by word count or current position.
  2. Retire word count and ranking position as standalone triage signals. Both looked meaningful in raw comparisons and both disappeared under controls; using them to prioritize review work would misallocate effort.
  3. Treat top-3-position pages as a watch list, not a safe list. They showed the highest raw decline rate, plausibly from a ceiling effect — worth light monitoring rather than urgent rewrite.
  4. Re-run this scoring on the full warehouse before operationalizing it. The modest lift and the 30k-row / 32-client scope make this a starting hypothesis, not a production-ready score.
